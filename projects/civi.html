Overview

We believe safety is a right for everyone. That's why Civi aligns information and technology to help individuals and communities stay better informed and safer.

Civi's goal is to become the number one security app in Latin America by empowering tens of millions of users to improve their personal security. At the time of this project, the company was focused on São Paulo, Brazil.

First wireframe

Around 03/2019 I started working at Civi. I began by creating the first wireframe of the app, based on a sketch the founder gave me. The goal was to build an MVP and launch the first version of the app as fast as possible.

Interface overtime

Between 03/2019 and 09/2022 the app evolved from MVP to a fully-fledged product. The interface changed gradually over time — here are a few key moments from that evolution.

Design system

I created the design system with the help of a second UX/UI designer. The master components helped the app grow consistently, with a strong visual identity — and saved a lot of development time.

Onboarding

The onboarding was one of the most elaborate flows in the app. We found that the drop-off rate during onboarding was very high, because we were requesting a lot of personal information — home address, work address, device location permission, and more.

Together with another UX designer, I worked on improving the onboarding flow to strike a better balance: collecting enough information about users while keeping the flow short and easy to digest.

App main flows

Map, search bar, save places, add contacts and settings.

Pending permissions and settings

The value of Civi depends heavily on the user's device permissions. The app needs users to allow access to their location, enable notifications, save their favorite places, and add contacts.

We noticed that many users ended the onboarding flow without completing these settings, and the app wasn't able to deliver its full value — resulting in low user retention.

To address this, we created a pending-settings carousel and a settings review flow. These helped draw attention to the importance of each setting and explained how it works to keep the user safer.

Pending permissions and settings

The value of Civi depends heavily on users adding contacts — inviting friends and family to see their location, receive alerts of occurrences nearby, and get automatic notifications when they arrive at or leave a saved place. However, a brief data analysis showed a low rate of sent contact invitations, and an even lower rate of accepted invites.

In user interviews, we found that many people went through onboarding without reading the explanations or trying to understand how the app worked — they wanted to finish registering as fast as possible to explore the app in practice, and ended onboarding without any motivation to invite others.

To solve this, we created Civiana — a virtual contact that works as an example of a contact already added in the app. Through her, you can see data such as her location, distance, battery level, and nearby occurrences. We also included her home and work addresses to show how the auto-check-in feature works.

This way, users could understand the value of adding contacts in practice. As a result, we saw a significant increase in the number of contact invitations sent.

Event icons redesign

Problem

Initially, we had assigned a unique icon to each of the over 80 event types, resulting in highly detailed illustrations. After a few tests, we noticed that many users found these icons hard to understand, since they appeared very small on the map.

We also had a problem with color: we used blue, gray, yellow, and red backgrounds to show how recent an event was. Since the icons were colorful too, it wasn't easy to associate a meaning with the background colors — rather than being helpful, the icons ended up visually distracting on the map.

Goals

  • Simplify the event icons, making them more intuitive and accessible.
  • Make the pins on the map clear and easy to understand.
  • Create simple filters for notifications by event type.

Process

I used the card sorting method to organize information into categories that are intuitive and meaningful to the target audience.

Participants are given a set of cards with items written on them and asked to sort them into groups based on their understanding of the relationships between the items. This informs the design and structure of a product, making it easier for users to find what they're looking for and understand the information presented to them.

Dynamic instructions

The participants were asked to do the following:

  1. Read the post-its one by one.
  2. Organize them into groups that make sense to you, by dragging the cards to the columns on the side. There's no right or wrong — follow your intuition.
  3. You can move cards between categories if you change your mind during the process.
  4. Use as many categories as you like — you don't need to fill every column. Duplicate the last one if needed. Colors are for visual clarity only.
  5. Name each category as it makes sense to you.

Gathering and analysing results

I collected all the results and put them together in a big matrix, then looked for patterns between the categories.

Card sorting result

At the end of the analysis, I arrived at 19 event categories and suggested monochromatic icons for each one.

Testing new icons with users

I applied all the new icons to a map simulation, then interviewed a few people about what they thought each icon meant, without giving much context.

I also created a poll on Slack with icon alternatives for the most difficult event categories, and my team helped define them.

User generated content (UGC)

Publication of events on the app.

Problem

Previously, we directed users to our WhatsApp central to report events for our team to validate and publish in the app. This sent users out of the app, generating disbelief; few people reported events, and the information we received was incomplete and lacked supporting media for validation.

Goal

Create an in-app flow for users to provide relevant information about accidents, crimes, and other incidents.

Benchmark research

To better understand UGC flows for event reporting, we ran a competitive audit of direct competitors like So Safe and SP + Segura. Since their UX wasn't optimal, we also looked at indirect competitors — Instagram, WhatsApp, TikTok, Facebook, and Airbnb — for inspiration.

UGC first version

We initially built a first version of the UGC flow for event reporting, but after launch we saw an even lower number of submissions. Analysis of the Firebase events showed many users dropping off during the flow, particularly at the upload-media step.

User interviews

To address these issues, we researched users who had successfully completed the UGC flow, to understand their experience and real-life scenarios worth considering in the redesign. We also researched users who dropped off during the flow, to understand why and gather feedback.

New flow advantages

We applyed several changes to the UGC event submission flow based on the most important feedbacks received during the user interviews. Here are some advantages of the new flow compared to the previous one:

Simplicity and speed

The old flow had 7 steps, the new one only 3 — sending anonymously, selecting date and time, and providing a description are all consolidated into a single screen.

Location tracking

The new flow automatically detects the user's current location and shows it on a map, removing the need to type an address — especially helpful when reporting something you witnessed on the street and aren't sure of the exact address.

Audio recording

Users can record audio to describe what happened, saving the time and effort of typing a description.

More intuitive flow

Some users didn't know they could report events through the app, so the first step now clearly shows all the available event types.

"Save Places" — discovery process

How we created the "Save Places" feature in the Civi app.

1. Interview script

For these interviews we focused on learning more about our most active users — when they used the app, how often, and for what purpose. At the time, we were also considering a map filter by event type, so we included a question about which event types and features they'd like to see in future versions.

Interview questions:

  • What do you do for a living?
  • How did you find out about Civi?
  • How has your experience with the product been so far?
  • Has the app ever crashed for you?
  • Do you feel safer with the app?
  • Have you been receiving notifications from Civi?
  • What event types do you think are relevant for you? Why?
  • What event types do you think are irrelevant? Why?
  • Is there any feature you'd like the app to have?

2. User interviews

About 15 active users were interviewed for around 10 minutes each. A document with each interview's questions, replies, and main insights was generated per interview.

3. Data compilation

From the interview documents, I built a spreadsheet with the key insights, feature suggestions, and the relevant/irrelevant event types mentioned

From them I generated three charts: the most relevant event types, the most irrelevant event types, and the most-mentioned feature suggestions.

4. Data analysis and conclusion

These interviews showed people were interested in knowing what was happening around their loved ones — beyond alerts about their own location, they wanted to know what was happening around their homes, workplaces, and other frequented places, and wanted a way to add other people and know that family and friends were safe too.

Besides that, over 23% of interviewees mentioned wanting to set up notifications by maximum distance or by event type — they were unhappy about receiving notifications for things happening far away, or about events they didn't care about.

5. Interactive prototype & user test

We built an interactive prototype to validate the feature with users, which let us apply improvements and corrections before shipping to production.

6. Breaking the feature into smaller versions

We fragmented this feature into four smaller versions, allowing a faster launch and giving us extra validation with users throughout implementation.
