Overview

Civi believes safety is a right for everyone, and aligns information and technology to help individuals and communities stay better informed and safer.

Civi's goal is to become the number one security app in Latin America by empowering tens of millions of users to improve their personal security. At the time of this project, the company was focused on São Paulo, Brazil.

First wireframe

Giovana made the first wireframe of the Civi app based on a sketch the founder gave her.

The goal was to build an MVP and launch the first version of the app as fast as possible.

This first version included a COVID-19 tracker. This function was descontinued after 2021.

"Save places" discovery process (2021)

How the "Save Places" feature came to be in the Civi app.

1. Interview plan

Goal

The goal was to make sure efforts were concentrated on developing the right features in the app, to make it more useful and attractive for users.

There were also a lot of internal discussions about what kind of events would be relevant to display in the map and send alert notifications.

Target group

These interviews focused on learning more about the app's most active users — when they used the app, how often, and for what purposes.

Interview questions

  • What do you do for a living?
  • How did you find out about Civi?
  • How has your experience with the product been so far?
  • Has the app ever crashed for you?
  • Do you feel safer with the app?
  • Have you been receiving notifications from Civi?
  • What event types do you think are relevant for you? Why?
  • What event types do you think are irrelevant? Why?
  • Is there any functionality you would like the app to have?

2. User interviews

About 15 active users were interviewed for around 10 minutes each. A document with each interview's questions, replies, and main insights was generated per interview.

3. Data compilation

From the interview documents, Giovana built a spreadsheet with the key insights, feature suggestions, and the relevant/irrelevant event types mentioned.

From them, she generated three charts: the most relevant event types, the most irrelevant event types, and the most-mentioned feature suggestions.

4. Data analysis and conclusion

These interviews showed people were interested in knowing what was happening around their loved ones — beyond alerts about their own location, they wanted to know what was happening around their homes, workplaces, and other frequented places, and wanted a way to add other people and know that family and friends were safe too.

Besides that, over 23% of interviewees mentioned wanting to set up notifications by maximum distance or by event type — they were unhappy about receiving notifications for things happening far away, or about events they didn't care about.

5. Interactive prototype & user test

The team built an interactive prototype to validate the feature with users, which allowed them to apply improvements and corrections before shipping to production.

6. Breaking the feature into smaller versions

The team fragmented this feature into four smaller versions, allowing a faster launch and giving them extra validation with users throughout implementation.

Onboarding redesign (2022)

Problem

The onboarding was one of the most elaborate flows in the app. The team found that the drop-off rate during onboarding was very high, because the app was requesting a lot of personal information — home address, work address, device location permission, and more.

Solution

Together with another UX designer, Giovana worked on improving the onboarding flow to strike a better balance: collecting enough information about users while keeping the flow short and easy to digest.

Pending permissions and settings (2022)

Problem

The value of Civi depends heavily on the user's device permissions. The app needs users to allow access to their location, enable notifications, save their favorite places, and add contacts.

The team noticed that many users ended the onboarding flow without completing these settings, and the app wasn't able to deliver its full value — resulting in low user retention.

Solution

To address this, the team created a pending-settings carousel and a settings review flow. These helped draw attention to the importance of each setting and explained how it works to keep the user safer.

Virtual contact "Civiana" (2023)

Problem

The value of Civi depends heavily on users adding contacts — inviting friends and family to see their location, receive alerts of occurrences nearby, and get automatic notifications when they arrive at or leave a saved place. However, a brief data analysis showed a low rate of sent contact invitations, and an even lower rate of accepted invites.

In user interviews, the team found that many people went through onboarding without reading the explanations or trying to understand how the app worked — they wanted to finish registering as fast as possible to explore the app in practice, and ended onboarding without any motivation to invite others.

Solution

To solve this, the team created Civiana — a virtual contact that works as an example of a contact already added in the app. Through her, you can see data such as her location, distance, battery level, and nearby occurrences. Her home and work addresses were also included to show how the auto-check-in feature works.

This way, users could understand the value of adding contacts in practice. As a result, the team saw a significant increase in the number of contact invitations sent.

Places suggestions (2023)

Problem

The team built an auto-check-in and auto-check-out function that sends notifications when a contact arrives at or leaves a saved place — for example, "Mary arrived at home" or "John left work." The problem was that even though users were adding contacts, they weren't adding places for those contacts. For example, a user might add their partner but never add their partner's office — meaning they couldn't use the auto check-in/out feature for that place.

Solution

A list of suggested places was added, displaying the places from a user's added contacts.

Event icons redesign (2022)

Problem

Initially, the app had a unique icon assigned to each of the over 80 event types, resulting in highly detailed illustrations. After a few tests, the team noticed that many users found these icons hard to understand, since they appeared very small on the map.

There was also a problem with color: the app used blue, gray, yellow, and red backgrounds to show how recent an event was. Since the icons were colorful too, it wasn't easy to associate a meaning with the background colors — rather than being helpful, the icons ended up visually distracting on the map.

Goals

  • Simplify the event icons, making them more intuitive and accessible.
  • Make the pins on the map clear and easy to understand.
  • Create simple filters for notifications by event type.

Process

Giovana used the card sorting method to organize information into categories that are intuitive and meaningful to the target audience.

Participants are given a set of cards with items written on them and asked to sort them into groups based on their understanding of the relationships between the items. This informs the design and structure of a product, making it easier for users to find what they're looking for and understand the information presented to them.

Dynamic instructions

The participants were asked to do the following:

  1. Read the post-its one by one.
  2. Organize them into groups that make sense to you, by dragging the cards to the columns on the side. There's no right or wrong — follow your intuition.
  3. You can move cards between categories if you change your mind during the process.
  4. Use as many categories as you like — you don't need to fill every column. Duplicate the last one if needed. Colors are for visual clarity only.
  5. Name each category as it makes sense to you.

Gathering and analysing results

She collected all the results and put them together in a big matrix, then looked for patterns between the categories.

Card sorting result

At the end of the analysis, Giovana arrived at 19 event categories and suggested monochromatic icons for each one.

Testing new icons with users

Giovana applied all the new icons to a map simulation, then interviewed a few people about what they thought each icon meant, without giving much context.

She also created a poll on Slack with icon alternatives for the most difficult event categories, and the team helped define them.

Event submission flow (2022)

Publication of events on the app. User generated content (UGC).

Problem

Previously, users were directed to Civi's WhatsApp central to report events for the team to validate and publish in the app. This sent users out of the app, generating disbelief; few people reported events, and the information received was incomplete and lacked supporting media for validation.

Goal

Create an in-app flow for users to provide relevant information about accidents, crimes, and other incidents.

Benchmark research

To better understand UGC flows for event reporting, the team ran a competitive audit of direct competitors like So Safe and SP + Segura. Since their UX wasn't optimal, they also looked at indirect competitors — Instagram, WhatsApp, TikTok, Facebook, and Airbnb — for inspiration.

UGC first version

The team initially built a first version of the UGC flow for event reporting, but after launch, submissions dropped even lower. Analysis of the Firebase events showed many users dropping off during the flow, particularly at the upload-media step.

User interviews

To address these issues, the team researched users who had successfully completed the UGC flow, to understand their experience and real-life scenarios worth considering in the redesign. They also researched users who dropped off during the flow, to understand why and gather feedback.

New flow advantages

The team applied several changes to the UGC event submission flow based on the most important feedback received during the user interviews. Here are some advantages of the new flow compared to the previous one:

Simplicity and speed

The old flow had 7 steps, the new one only 3 — sending anonymously, selecting date and time, and providing a description are all consolidated into a single screen.

Location tracking

The new flow automatically detects the user's current location and shows it on a map, removing the need to type an address — especially helpful when reporting something you witnessed on the street and aren't sure of the exact address.

Audio recording

Users can record audio to describe what happened, saving the time and effort of typing a description.

More intuitive flow

Some users didn't know they could report events through the app, so the first step now clearly shows all the available event types.

Premium plan & free trial (2023)

One of the last major additions to Civi was a paid premium plan, "Civi Protege." The idea was to allow only premium users to add contacts.

Free users would get alerts close to themselves and their saved places. Premium users could also see their contacts' location, receive notifications every time a contact arrived at or left a specific place (home, office, or school, for example), and access their contacts' location history and battery level.

The team also built a free trial flow so free users could try the paid features for two weeks.

The end of Civi

Around June 2023, Civi's founders shared that they hadn't been able to raise a new round of investment, and the newly launched premium plan wasn't generating enough revenue to keep the company running. Shortly after, they decided to close it down.

Looking back, a few things stand out:

Monetization came too late

The team wanted to build a large, popular app before introducing a paid plan. By the time Civi Protege launched, it was already too late to change the company's trajectory.

Acquisition outpaced retention

The team invested heavily in acquiring new users while retention stayed low, so a large part of that investment went toward users who left the app quickly.

The team scaled faster than the roadmap

Many people were hired in a short period, and a lot of time went into onboarding instead of the product. The new hires brought great ideas, but the team wasn't ready to act on them yet — a smaller team with a sharper focus on the core experience might have served them better.

Discovery took priority over shipping basics

A lot of time went into research and considering elaborate features before the basic experience was solid. The onboarding flow, for example, was buggy and visually inconsistent because the design system took too long to become a priority — and that alone caused many new users to drop off before finishing it.

This experience taught Giovana to weigh business fundamentals — monetization, retention, team pacing — as closely as she weighs usability and visual consistency. It's a lesson she carries into every project since.
